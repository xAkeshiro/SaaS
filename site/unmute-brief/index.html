Unmute is the daily "say it out loud" habit that made Speak a $1B company, pointed at a bigger market than language learning: the conversations people are actually afraid of.

The demand is loudly documented (65% of Gen Z are uncomfortable calling a stranger), the technology arrived this year (full-duplex voice that interrupts and pushes back), the enterprise side of the category is already funded (Second Nature $22M, Hyperbound $15M, Big Interview in 700+ universities), and no consumer brand owns it. The one review of the space that exists says it in one sentence: "the hard talk with your mother, your partner, or your shut-down teenager falls in the gap" between workplace trainers and open-ended chatbots.

Why now

  • The problem is universal and measured. 65% of Gen Z say calling a stranger makes them uncomfortable; 75% feel anxious about phone calls; comfort with calls falls from 67% of Boomers to 49% of Millennials to 33% of Gen Z. 37% of adults 18–25 report significant anxiety, the highest of any adult group. 57% of college students report loneliness. "Gen Z can't talk to people" is a recurring press genre.
  • The technology arrived this year. Sesame's Maya and Miles showed full-duplex, interruptible, emotionally responsive voice; OpenAI's realtime models and ElevenLabs Agents made it an API at $0.02–0.11 a minute; average response latency is now under 400 ms. Practicing with a stiff chatbot was pointless. Practicing with something that sighs, interrupts, and warms up when you do is not.
  • The mechanic is proven at scale. Speak's thesis is "get people to speak out loud as much as possible," rewarded daily; users practice 5–10x more than on other apps. Duolingo, with 100M+ users, has narrowed its paid tier to exactly two features: Roleplay and Video Call.
  • The category is funded on the enterprise side and empty on the consumer side. Second Nature raised a $22M Series B (Oct 2025, Zoom participating); Hyperbound raised a $15M Series A (Sep 2025, YC and Peak XV); Yoodli sells interview and communication practice to companies; Big Interview is licensed by 700+ universities. Nobody has built the consumer habit for the conversations that are not a job interview.
  • The anti-companion moment. Character.ai is under state regulation and wrongful-death settlements; Friend is a punchline; the AI companion market is projected at $1.8B and is the thing regulators and parents are worried about. A coach whose stated goal is to make you need it less is the voice-AI story journalists want to write right now.

The product

  1. Rehearse. Pick a scenario from the library or describe your own ("my landlord won't return my deposit"). The AI plays the other side with a consistent personality, a voice, a mood and a difficulty from kind to hostile. Full-duplex: it interrupts, sighs, goes quiet, pushes back, and moves when you make a calm, specific ask.
  2. Debrief. Instantly after: what worked, where you folded, how long you took to get to the ask, filler words, pace, a replay with highlights, and the two sentences to say next time. A confidence trend over weeks. The AI remembers your patterns: "you apologize before every ask."
  3. The daily rep. Three minutes, streaks, a scenario picked from what you have coming up. Connect your calendar and "interview Thursday" becomes Tuesday's rep.
  4. Real mode. Before a real call: a 60-second warmup and a cue card. After: a debrief from your own notes. Unmute never listens to real calls; phones do not allow it and nobody would trust it if they did.
  5. Together. Practice rooms where a friend plays the recruiter while the AI coaches, or the AI plays the panel while friends rate you. Dares with shareable clips. The before-and-after clip of your first call next to your tenth, which is the whole pitch in fifteen seconds.
  6. Guardrails. A coach, not therapy: crisis language routes to real resources; no romantic roleplay; every persona is clearly synthetic; rehearsals are private and never used for training.

The voice is not the product. The structure, the scoring, the memory of your patterns and the habit are the product. That is how Speak survived ChatGPT, and it is the answer to "what if OpenAI adds a practice mode."

A free user who reps every day costs $2–10 a month on today's prices, which is why the free tier is one rep and the hard paywall sits after the first debrief, where consumer AI apps convert best (10.7% versus 2.1% for soft paywalls). Plus at $99 a year against roughly $25–40 a year of inference for a heavy user is a healthy margin, and voice prices have fallen about 50% in the last twelve months.

Scale check: 20 million US college students plus everyone with an interview, a landlord or a manager. One million paying users at the annual price is $99M ARR, Speak's scale, before the Teams tier.

Launch plan

  1. Interview season is the wedge. Campus recruiting runs September through November. The first campaign is "rehearse your interview tonight," seeded through the communities you can reach, with career centers offered the Teams tier free for the season in exchange for a case study. Big Interview proves they buy this; Unmute is the one students will actually open.
  2. The before-and-after clip. Every user can export their first rehearsal next to their tenth, voice-changed if they want. That clip is the content engine. Seed it with 20–50 creators on the "phone anxiety" and "Gen Z can't make calls" formats, paid per view, the Rizz and Cal AI playbook.
  3. Dares. Weekly public dares ("call the pizza place and negotiate a discount," "ask for the manager's name and use it") with a leaderboard of completed clips. Low stakes, high shareability, and every dare ends in a real conversation.
  4. The anti-companion press story. Launch with a stated position: no romantic roleplay, no endless chat, sessions end with a real conversation to go have. Pitch it as the answer to the companion backlash, with the retention data to back it.
  5. Practice rooms bring the second user. Every room needs a friend; every friend gets their own daily rep. Referral credit is a free month of Plus for both.
  6. YC. Apply with week-4 retention, paid conversion, the clip that traveled, and one career center on Teams. The thesis, the comparable and the timing are already YC-shaped; the numbers are what is missing.

Risks and mitigations

  • ChatGPT adds a practice mode. Speak survived ChatGPT by being structured, scored and habitual. The debrief, the pattern memory, the curriculum and the rooms are the product, not the voice.
  • Voice cost. Falling fast and already fine at the paid tier; the free tier is capped at one rep, and the mini model serves it.
  • Cringe. Talking to your phone out loud in a dorm is awkward. Speak's 15M downloads say people do it; headphones, a text-first on-ramp and private rooms lower the bar.
  • Safety. Vulnerable users will bring real fear to it. Clinical advisors, crisis routing, no romantic scenarios, clear synthetic disclosure, and a hard line in the copy: practice, not therapy.
  • Retention. Skill apps retain when there is a next real conversation to prepare for; the calendar hook and the daily rep exist for that reason, and week-4 retention is the kill-or-scale number.
  • Trademark. "Unmute" is a common word in software; clear it in the app-software class before the launch, with "Say It" and "Out Loud" as fallbacks.
