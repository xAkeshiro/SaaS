Bloc is a collective-leverage engine: AI that clusters people with the same grievance into a bloc, models what it costs the counterparty to fight, and executes the cheapest path to a remedy across five channels at a scale no human organizer can match.

It is the consumer side of a market that today exists only on the law-firm side. Darrow sells AI-found class actions to 80 firms, is cash-flow positive, and forecasts $120M of revenue in 2026. Nobody organizes the people who were harmed. That is the whole opportunity.

Why now

Companies built their defenses assuming complainants are alone and paperwork is expensive. Both assumptions broke in the last eighteen months.

  • Mass arbitration works. Of 26 mass arbitrations tracked in 2025, none was dismissed on the merits; all settled, were withdrawn, or died on fees. Meta paid $64.5M after 100,000 Instagram claims. Valve dropped its arbitration clause entirely and sued the firm that recruited claimants. Ten thousand claims cost a company nearly $6M in AAA fees before anyone argues the merits.
  • Companies answered with friction built for humans. Amazon's terms effective 14 Aug 2026 require a Notice of Dispute and 60 days of "good-faith negotiation" before arbitration, batch 25+ coordinated claims into the JAMS mass procedure, and demand litigation-funding disclosure. Friction designed for humans is exactly what an AI removes.
  • The individual model died. FairShake automated one-person arbitration for a 20% cut and ceased operations. One person's $80 cable dispute cannot pay for a company. Four thousand people with the same dispute can.
  • The organizing instinct is already here, without tools. Philadelphia's tenant unions quadrupled since the pandemic; the student tenant-union movement is spreading; the first collectively bargained lease is being negotiated. Change.org has the numbers and no teeth.
  • Regulators are a pressure channel. Companies must respond to CFPB complaints within 15 days; the CFPB received 6.6M complaints in 2025, double the year before. Four hundred complaints about one practice in one week is a supervisory event; two are noise.

The mechanism

  1. Intake. A person describes what happened and uploads what they have. The AI extracts counterparty, category, the specific practice or clause, dates, amount, jurisdiction, evidence quality, and the contract terms that govern the dispute.
  2. The grievance graph. Claims cluster across all members by counterparty and pattern: the same fee, the same denial code, the same lease clause, the same building, the same defect. Each cluster is a candidate bloc with a live power score.
  3. The strategist. For each bloc, a planner models the counterparty's cost of fighting and picks the cheapest path to a remedy, in sequence, from the company's own terms, the regulators with jurisdiction, small-claims limits by state, and the outcome history of every previous bloc.
  4. Execution channels, each with its own legal rules. A collective demand copied to executives and, where lawful, reporters; a regulator swarm with an individually correct filing per member; a small-claims swarm, which no consumer contract can waive; pre-arbitration notices, 60-day trackers and evidence-ready claimant files handed to a partner firm for mass arbitration; class-action referral where no arbitration clause exists.
  5. The negotiation agent. With members' authorization, one agent negotiates one settlement for the whole bloc: refunds to all members, a fee removed, a policy changed. The company saves the cost of fighting; members get money without hiring anyone.
  6. Outcomes train the strategist. Every result feeds the model of what works against which company through which channel. This is the flywheel.

Why numbers work and individuals do not

Every earlier consumer product in this space, from DoNotPay to FairShake, helped one person at a time. Bloc's unit is the bloc. That single change is what makes the economics, the leverage and the moat work.

Where it spreads

Students are the most over-charged and least-organized population in the country, and their grievances are naturally shared: the same housing complex, meal plan, mandatory fee, textbook auto-billing, parking machine and tuition payment-plan fee. Each is a bloc that one student can start and the whole campus can join in a day. The student tenant-union movement is already spreading with no software behind it; Bloc gives it a spine.

From there, blocs spread by counterparty rather than by geography: everyone with the same insurer denial, the same telecom fee, the same airline, the same subscription trap. A company's own customer base becomes the community. Every bloc page is a share link with a live member count and a clear ask.

Compliance by design

  • No unauthorized practice of law. Bloc drafts documents members file themselves and explains options in plain language; individualized advice and representation come from marketplace firms who sign their own clients.
  • Lead generation, not fee-sharing. Firms pay flat or subscription fees regardless of outcome; no lawyer recommendations; state rules handled per state, including North Carolina's registration for interactive legal-document sites.
  • Good-faith claims only. Every member is a real, harmed party with evidence, scored for viability before any filing. This is the line between Bloc and the recruit-anyone tactic Valve sued over.
  • Truthful pressure. Scorecards and demands are evidence-based; no campaign runs on unverified claims.
  • Privacy. Denial letters and bills are sensitive: encryption, minimal retention, member-controlled sharing, no training on member documents.
  • Substantiated outcomes. Every published success statistic is logged and auditable.

Moats and risks

Moats

  • The grievance graph. The only dataset of who was harmed by what, with evidence, across companies. It compounds.
  • Outcome data. Which channel works against which company at what bloc size. Nobody else runs the experiment.
  • Execution depth. Hundreds of regulator formats, state small-claims rules and forum procedures, encoded and maintained. Boring, expensive, and exactly what stops a weekend clone.
  • Two-sided network. Firms come for organized blocs; members come because firms and outcomes are there.

Risks

  • Companies fight back with terms changes, suits like Valve's, and forum pressure. The ladder never depends on one channel, and regulators and small claims cannot be waived by contract.
  • Scrutiny of the platform itself: lead-gen ethics, claims-assistance rules, funding disclosure. Counsel from day one; the marketplace structured as advertising, not referral.
  • Slow money. Legal outcomes take months; firm subscriptions and organizer software pay from month one.
  • Bad actors. Evidence scoring, identity verification, rate limits on new members' influence.
  • The AI is wrong about the law. Channel rules are reviewed logic, not free generation; a lawyer reviews each playbook; members see sources.
