## 2. Why now, in one paragraph

Companies built their defenses assuming complainants are alone and paperwork is expensive. Both assumptions just broke. On the company side, mass arbitration has become the one tactic that reliably forces settlements: of 26 mass arbitrations tracked in 2025, none was dismissed on the merits; all settled, were withdrawn, or died on fees; Meta paid $64.5M after 100,000 Instagram claims; Valve dropped its arbitration clause and sued the law firm that recruited claimants. Companies responded with friction: Amazon's terms effective 14 Aug 2026 require a Notice of Dispute and 60 days of "good-faith negotiation" before arbitration, batch 25+ coordinated claims into the JAMS mass procedure, and demand litigation-funding disclosure. Friction designed for humans is exactly what an AI removes. On the consumer side, FairShake, which automated individual arbitration for a 20% cut, ceased operations: one person's $80 cable dispute cannot pay for a company. A bloc of 4,000 people with the same dispute can. And the organizing instinct is already here without tools: Philadelphia's tenant unions have quadrupled since the pandemic, the student tenant-union movement is spreading, and the first collectively bargained lease is being negotiated.

## 3. The mechanism

1. **Intake.** A person describes what happened and uploads what they have (a bill, a denial letter, a lease, screenshots, a chargeback record). The AI extracts a structured claim: counterparty, category, the specific practice or clause, dates, amount, jurisdiction, evidence quality, and the contractual terms that govern the dispute (arbitration clause, class waiver, notice requirements).
2. **The grievance graph.** Claims are clustered across all members by counterparty and pattern: the same fee, the same denial code, the same lease clause, the same building, the same product defect. Each cluster is a candidate bloc with a live "bloc power" score: members × claim value × evidence quality × legal viability × counterparty exposure.
3. **The strategist.** For each bloc, an AI planner models the counterparty's cost of fighting and picks the cheapest path to a remedy, in sequence. Its inputs are the company's own terms (does arbitration apply? which forum? what pre-dispute steps?), the regulators with jurisdiction and their response rules (CFPB companies must respond within 15 days; state AGs mediate; DOT, HHS, FCC, state insurance departments each have formats), small-claims limits by state, and the outcome history of every previous bloc against that company or its peers.
4. **Execution channels, each with its own legal rules.**
   - *Collective demand:* one letter, signed by N members, with a deadline and a specific remedy, delivered to the company's legal and executive contacts and, where lawful, copied to journalists (Claimable proved copying executives and reporters works).
   - *Regulator swarm:* an individually correct complaint filed for every member in the format each regulator requires. Four hundred CFPB complaints about one practice in one week is a supervisory event; two are noise.
   - *Small-claims swarm:* small claims is exempt from arbitration in nearly every consumer contract, including Amazon's new one. The AI prepares each member's filing for their own county; members file themselves.
   - *Pre-arbitration and mass arbitration:* the AI generates each member's Notice of Dispute, tracks every 60-day window, prepares settlement-conference positions, and hands a complete, evidence-ready claimant file to a partner law firm that files the mass arbitration. Ten thousand claims cost a company nearly $6M in AAA fees before anyone argues the merits.
   - *Class action referral:* where no arbitration clause exists, the bloc file goes to firms through the marketplace, the mirror image of what Darrow sells to those firms today.
5. **The negotiation agent.** With members' authorization, one agent negotiates one settlement for the whole bloc: refunds to all members, a fee removed, a policy changed. The company saves the cost of fighting; members get money without hiring anyone.
6. **Outcomes feed the strategist.** Every result (offer, refund, silence, settlement, policy change) trains the model of what works against which company through which channel. This is the data flywheel: the more blocs run, the better the next bloc's plan.

## 5. Where it spreads: the campus wedge

Students are the most over-charged and least-organized population in the country, and their grievances are naturally shared: the same housing complex, the same meal plan, the same mandatory fee, the same textbook auto-billing, the same parking ticket machine, the same tuition payment-plan fee. Each is a bloc that a single student can start and the whole campus can join in a day. The student tenant-union movement is already spreading with no software behind it. Bloc gives it a spine: verified members, pooled evidence, a strategist, and a path to a collectively bargained lease.

From there, blocs spread by counterparty rather than by geography: everyone with the same insurer denial, the same telecom fee, the same airline, the same app subscription trap. A company's own customer base becomes the community.

## 6. Compliance by design

- **No unauthorized practice of law.** Bloc drafts documents members file themselves and explains options in plain language. Individualized legal advice and representation come from marketplace firms who sign their own clients.
- **Lead generation, not fee-sharing.** Firms pay flat or subscription fees regardless of outcome; Bloc does not recommend specific lawyers; state-specific rules (and North Carolina registration for interactive legal-document sites) are handled per state.
- **Good-faith claims only.** Every bloc member is a real, harmed party with evidence; the AI scores viability before any filing. This is the difference between Bloc and the recruit-anyone tactic Valve sued over.
- **Truthful public pressure.** Scorecards and demands are evidence-based and factual; no campaign runs on unverified claims. Anti-SLAPP protection is real but not a license.
- **Privacy.** Denial letters and medical bills are sensitive; encryption, minimal retention, member-controlled sharing, and no training on member documents.
- **Substantiated outcomes.** Every success statistic Bloc publishes is logged and auditable, because the FTC is watching AI claims.

## 7. Moats

1. **The grievance graph.** The only dataset of who has been harmed by what, with evidence, across companies. It compounds.
2. **Outcome data.** Which channel works against which company at what bloc size. Nobody else runs the experiment.
3. **Execution depth.** Hundreds of regulator formats, state small-claims rules, arbitration forum procedures and pre-dispute requirements, all encoded and maintained. Boring, expensive, and exactly what stops a weekend clone.
4. **Two-sided network.** Firms come for organized blocs; members come because firms and outcomes are there.

## 8. Risks

- **Companies fight back.** Expect terms changes, suits like Valve's, and pressure on forums. Mitigation: good-faith-only membership, evidence standards, and a remedy ladder that does not depend on any single channel; the small-claims and regulator channels cannot be waived by contract.
- **Regulatory scrutiny of the platform itself.** Lead-gen ethics, claims-assistance rules, litigation-funding disclosure. Mitigation: the compliance design above and counsel from day one; structure the firm marketplace as advertising, not referral.
- **Slow money.** Legal outcomes take months. Mitigation: firm subscriptions and organizer SaaS pay from month one; success fees compound later.
- **Bad actors.** Fraudulent claims poison a bloc. Mitigation: evidence scoring, identity verification, and rate limits on new members' influence.
- **The AI is wrong about the law.** Mitigation: every channel's rules are encoded as reviewed logic, not free generation; a lawyer reviews the strategist's playbooks per channel; members see sources.
